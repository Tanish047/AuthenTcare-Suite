MAP

Orientation: where this standard sits

Before any clause, fix the one thing this document does: it protects the patient from hazardous substances carried to them by the gas stream — not by touching a surface, but by being inspired (breathed in).

In plain English

ISO 10993 covers materials that touch the patient. But the inside of a breathing tube never touches you — the gas that flows through it does. ISO 10993 does not sufficiently address that pathway, so ISO 18562 was written specifically for the surfaces that gas passes over on its way to the lungs.

FOREWORDWhat this second edition changed

Prepared by ISO/TC 121 (Anaesthetic and respiratory equipment), SC 3 (Lung ventilators and related equipment), in collaboration with CEN/TC 215 under the Vienna Agreement. This second edition cancels and replaces ISO 18562-1:2017, which has been technically revised. The eight listed changes:

  1. added informative mapping annexes to relevant regulatory requirements (Annexes B and C);
  2. clarified terms and definitions;
  3. expanded the patient groups to include premature, small child, child and adolescent;
  4. introduction of inhalation dose (the new term 3.14);
  5. the threshold of toxicological concern is changed;
  6. expanded the range of volatile organic substances that are tested;
  7. clarified the appropriate breathing gas volumes to be used in testing for VOS; and
  8. clarified the appropriate breathing gas volumes to be used in the analysis.
Why 7 and 8 are separate One is about the volume you push through the device on the test bench; the other is about the volume you multiply a measured concentration by to get a dose. Different numbers, different purposes — hence two bullets.

INTROWhy the ISO 18562 series exists

  • The document applies the best-known science to improve patient safety by addressing the risk of potentially hazardous substances being conveyed to the patient by the gas stream.
  • It combines review and evaluation of existing data from all sources with, where necessary, the selection and application of additional tests.
  • In general the ISO 10993 series covers biological evaluation of medical devices, but it does not sufficiently address gas pathways.
  • The historical problem: before this document existed, some authorities having jurisdiction interpreted ISO 10993-1:2009, Table A.1 to mean that because gas pathway materials form “indirect contact” with the patient, they should face tests equivalent to tissue-contacting parts. That interpretation leads to tests not optimized for gas pathways — and to possible hazards not being detected.
  • The permission slip: ISO 10993-1:2018 says it is not intended to provide a rigid set of test methods, and that where an application warrants it, experts may establish specific tests and criteria in a product-specific vertical standard. ISO 18562 is that vertical standard.
  • It guides a plan that minimizes the number and exposure of test animals by preferring chemical constituent testing and in vitro models.
The TTC leap — and its exceptions

The initial version deliberately covered only the most commonly found potentially harmful substances, to get a working document published. With the TTC (threshold of toxicological concern) approach, this document has the potential to assess the safety of essentially any compound released from a respiratory device's gas pathway — with very few exceptions, e.g. PCBs and dioxins — and not just the commonly found ones.

The Introduction's own honesty clause

ISO 18562-1 does not address all possible biological hazards that can be associated with gas pathways. Other, additional evaluations can be appropriate, and those evaluations can require further risk control before finishing the biological evaluation. This is the same caveat as footnote “a” on Figure 2.

Named as future work Future parts might address other aspects of biological testing, including contamination arising from drugs and anaesthetic agents added to the gas stream, and potential contamination by emission of inorganic gases such as ozone, CO, CO2 and NO. EXAMPLE

Meet the device we will use everywhere

A medical oxygen-delivery system: a cylinder supplies O2, a valve/regulator controls flow, a tube carries the gas, and a mask delivers it to the patient, who inspires it.

For our device — what counts as the gas pathway
  • Inside the valve/regulator (downstream ports), the inner lumen of the tube, and the inner surface of the mask — every interior surface the O2 flows over before it is inspired.
  • The pathway is bounded by the ports through which gas enters the device (3.11, Note 1).
Out of scope for our device (do not evaluate under 18562)
  • The oxygen itself from the cylinder. Contamination already present in gas supplied from gas sources is explicitly not addressed — the standard's own EXAMPLE names medical gas pipeline systems including the non-return valves in pipeline outlets, outlets of pressure regulators connected to or integral with a cylinder, and room air taken into the device.
  • The mask's patient-contacting cushion on the face — direct contact goes to the ISO 10993 series.
  • Biological hazards arising from mechanical damage, and abnormal use.
§3.11

The gas pathway — the concept the whole standard turns on

Definition, paraphrased Gas pathway = interior surfaces over which gases or liquids pass that can be inspired. Note 1: it is bounded by the ports through which gases or liquids enter the device or accessory, and can include the patient interface or the interior surfaces of enclosures in contact with inspirable gases or liquids. Note 2: it can include some surfaces in the expiratory pathway. Note 3: patient contact surfaces such as the outer surface of a tracheal tube or the cushion of a mask are evaluated to the ISO 10993 series.

Everything downstream in the standard — which tests, which limits, which patient group — hangs off which surfaces are in the gas pathway and how long the patient is exposed. Get the pathway boundary right first.

The standard's own three examples EXAMPLE 1 the ventilator breathing system, inlet filter, gas mixer, blower and internal piping. EXAMPLE 2 the enclosed chamber of an incubator including the mattress, or the inner surface of an oxygen hood. EXAMPLE 3 the inner surfaces of breathing tubes, tracheal tubes, masks and mouthpieces.
§1

Scope — what Part 1 does and does not do

This document specifies (six bullets)
  • the general principles governing biological evaluation, within a risk management process, of the gas pathways of a device, its parts or accessories intended to provide respiratory care or supply substances via the respiratory tract in all environments;
  • the general categorization of gas pathways based on the nature and duration of their contact with the gas stream;
  • the evaluation of existing relevant data from all sources;
  • the identification of gaps in the available data set on the basis of a risk analysis;
  • the identification of additional data sets necessary to analyse biological safety;
  • the assessment of the biological safety of the gas pathway.
Part 1 does NOT cover
  • biological hazards arising from mechanical damage;
  • biological evaluation of surfaces with direct contact with the patient or user → ISO 10993 series;
  • contamination already present in the gas supplied from the gas sources during normal use;
  • abnormal use — conscious/intentional action or inaction violating the IFU and beyond reasonable risk control by the manufacturer.
The lifetime sentence — read it twice

This document applies over the expected lifetime of the device when operated according to the instructions for use. That expressly includes degradation arising from exposure to environmental conditions as well as cleaning, disinfection and sterilisation (i.e. processing). It also includes use error (user action or inaction leading to an unintended or unexpected result). It does not include abnormal use.

Devices named in the scope Not limited to, but explicitly: ventilators; anaesthesia workstations including gas mixers; breathing systems; oxygen conserving equipment; oxygen concentrators; nebulizers; low-pressure hose assemblies; humidifiers; heat and moisture exchangers; respiratory gas monitors; respiration monitors; masks; medical respiratory personal protective equipment; mouth pieces; resuscitators; breathing tubes; breathing system filters; Y-pieces; and any breathing accessories intended for use with such devices. The enclosed chamber of an incubator, including the mattress, and the inner surface of an oxygen hood are gas pathways and are also addressed.
Division of labour with the other parts The other parts of ISO 18562 cover the specific tests for potentially hazardous substances added to the respirable gas stream and establish the acceptance criteria. Part 1 addresses contamination arising from the gas pathways within the device, which might then be conducted to the patient.
For our device In scope: PM, VOS and (if relevant) condensate leachables from the valve interior, tube lumen and mask interior. Out of scope: the purity of the bottled O2, and the mask cushion against the skin. If the tube and mask are reusable and get cleaned or disinfected between patients, that processing is squarely inside the lifetime sentence above.

The one-line distinction that unlocks Clauses 7 and 8 Data exists → TE. No data exists → TTC. And TI is the per-kilogram intermediate you multiply by body mass to get a TE. So the chain is always: toxicological data → TI (µg/kg/d) → × body mass → TE (µg/d) → compare against the measured dose (µg/d).

Reading Figure 2 in one breath

Specify the device and its durations → write the plan → for each of the three routes ask “is this contamination credible?” If no, supply a rationale and document the outcome (no test). If yes, condition, test, report, then compare with acceptance criteria. Criteria met → document. Not met → ask whether further risk control is practicable: yes loops you all the way back to the use specification, no means you document the outcome and carry it forward. Then write the report and ask whether the biological risk is acceptable: yes ends it, no sends you to benefit-risk analysis.

For our device — how the three credibility gates answer
  • ① PM: plastics in valve, tube and mask can shed particles → credible → run the lane, test to Part 2.
  • ② VOS: polymers off-gas → credible → run the lane, test to Part 3.
  • ③ Leachables in condensate: dry cylinder O2, no humidifier, no water to condense → not credible → supply rationale → document outcome. Add a bubble humidifier and this gate flips to credible.

4.1General

SHALL — a), b), c), d)
  • a) Biocompatibility evaluation of breathing gas pathways intended for healthcare use shall form part of a structured biocompatibility evaluation programme within a risk management process. ISO 10993-1 describes the process for devices in general; the ISO 18562 series describes it for breathing gas pathways; Figure 2 illustrates it.
  • b) All devices shall be evaluated for biocompatibility in normal use and normal condition.
  • c) All devices shall be evaluated for biocompatibility, but evaluation does not necessarily imply testing.
  • d) The evaluation shall be planned, carried out and documented by knowledgeable and experienced professionals.

4.1 e) — the evaluation programme shall include documented, informed decisions assessing the advantages, disadvantages and relevance of seven things

M O A C E H T
Material · Operation · Ageing · Cleaning · Environment · History · Toxicology — the seven items of 4.1 e) 1) to 7), in the standard's own order.
  1. Material — the physical and chemical characteristics of the various candidate materials over the expected lifetime of the device. NOTE 1: where this is already documented in the risk management file, it can be included by reference.
  2. Operation — effects of operation, handling and environment during use over the expected service life. NOTE 2: for a reusable device, wear and material degradation apply over the expected service life, not only the exposure period.
  3. Ageing — effects of aging and environment during storage over the shelf-life.
  4. Cleaning — effects of cleaning, disinfection and sterilisation (i.e. processing) in accordance with the accompanying information. Sub-item i) is mandatory: considerations shall include the agents' impact on degradation of gas pathway components and cleaning/sterilization methods that add potentially harmful substances to the gas pathway.
  5. Environment — effects from exposure to the rated environmental conditions.
  6. History — any history of human exposure data.
  7. Toxicology — any existing toxicology and other biocompatibility safety data on product and component materials, breakdown products and metabolites.
Three details inside MOACEHT that get dropped
  • Item 4 has a two-sided requirement: processing can degrade the pathway and can add harmful substances. Most summaries keep only the first half.
  • Items 1, 2 and 3 each attach to a different time window: expected lifetime, expected service life, shelf-life. They are not interchangeable.
  • Item 7 is not just “tox data” — it explicitly extends to breakdown products and metabolites.

4.1 f) — historical products, and the carve-back that bites

SHALL — read all four sentences
  1. This series shall not be used to mandate re-testing of historical products assessed previously using the appropriate edition of this document at the time of the assessment.
  2. Nevertheless, conformity to this new edition shall be shown, by providing a justification for omission of further testing.
  3. Where recommendations for endpoint assessment differ from prior published versions, a history of safe clinical use can be used to document why additional testing on a commercially-marketed device is not required.

A 71 % increase in breathed volume is a 71 % increase in inhalation dose, so this single choice can decide whether a compound passes.
For our device A home O2 device intended for adults uses the Adult row: 60 kg, 11,5 m³/d resting. If the IFU also claims infants, 6.2 b) forces the Infant row (3,5 kg, 2,3 m³/d) as the worst case. If it is a homecare device for an ambulant adult, Example 3 applies and the analysis volume becomes 19,7 m³/d.

Annexes A to D — all informative

Annex ARationale and guidance

A.1 General guidance: the annex gives the rationale for some requirements and is intended for people familiar with the subject but who did not participate in development. Understanding the reasons for the main requirements is considered essential for proper application, and preserving the rationale is expected to facilitate future revisions as clinical practice and technology change. A.2 is numbered to match the clauses it explains, so the numbering is deliberately not consecutive — it covers only 4.2, 6.1 and Clause 7.

A.2 on 4.2 — type tests

Variant read-across Some devices are provided in variants with small differences which have minor impact on their biological safety. It is therefore possible to use test results from one variant to evaluate other variants — but the subclause requires a justification for the choice of which variant to test.

A.2 on 6.1 b) 2) — the six patient strata

  • In this revision the committee further stratified the potential patient population, defining groups based on clinical presentation and body mass with defined risk profiles. Six strata: premature neonate, infant, small child, child, adolescent, adult.
  • For each group it defined an expected ideal body mass range, plus a default body mass (used for tolerable exposure calculations) and a default breathing volume (used for inhalation dose calculations).
  • Why the defaults are safe to generalise within a group: a premature neonate of 1,0 kg breathes twice the volume of the 0,5 kg default, giving twice the inhalation dose — but the same scaling also applies to the tolerable exposure, because the exposure dose applies to twice the body mass. So the concentration at which the tolerable intake is reached is the same for both patients.
  • The committee assumes the population with the lowest body mass is the most vulnerable, and in the absence of evidence to the contrary, limits based on it are conservatively protective for older and heavier groups.
The flow-rate caveat — the exception to “just use the smallest patient”

For some devices the flow rate through the device might not correspond directly with the patient's breathed volume — either because the patient breathes some gas independently of the device, or because the flow required to achieve therapy exceeds what is delivered to the lungs. If the ratio of flow rate to patient breathed volume is significantly different for different patient groups, it can be necessary to calculate the inhalation dose for all applicable patient groups.

So 6.2 b)'s “pick the smallest” shortcut rests on the ratio being similar across groups. High-flow devices, and devices delivering only part of the inspired gas, can break that assumption — and then the smallest patient is not automatically the worst case.

A.2 on Clause 7 — where 120 µg/d and 2 µg/m³ come from

  • The TTC level for unknown substances of 120 µg/d (limited and prolonged exposure) was taken from ICH M7:2023-09.
  • The inhalation TTC for prolonged (24 h to 30 d) exposure to VOSs released into the gas pathway is based on two candidate values:
    • the 5th percentile of a distribution of noncancer tolerable intake values derived from inhalation NOAEL (no observed adverse effect level) and LOAEL (lowest observed adverse effect level) values for exposure duration ≤ 30 d — 135 µg/d;
    • the acceptable intake of an individual mutagenic impurity in a pharmaceutical product with patient exposure ≤ 30 d — 120 µg/d.
    The lower of the two, 120 µg/d, was selected, and it is intended to be protective for both cancer and noncancer effects.
  • For long-term exposure the committee proposed 2 µg/m³. This is also the reporting limit for non-targeted compounds for indoor air according to ISO 16000, and is thus considered safe for permanent exposure to indoor air. That limit is considered too restrictive for limited and prolonged exposure, which is why the ICH M7 TTC levels are applied to those shorter durations instead.
  • The committee recognizes these limits can be adjusted in future as knowledge grows and analytical techniques improve, but judged them reasonably conservative on the balance of probabilities.
  • These limits apply only to substances added by the medical device to gases supplied to patients. They are not relevant to any other type of patient exposure.
The logic to keep The two defaults come from different worlds: 120 µg/d from pharmaceutical impurity practice (ICH M7), 2 µg/m³ from indoor air quality practice (ISO 16000). Short exposure borrows from pharma because the air-quality value is too strict for it; long exposure borrows from air quality because permanent exposure is exactly what that standard is built for. And the last bullet is a hard boundary: these numbers are only for what the device adds — never quote them as general inhalation limits.

Full walkthrough: our device through the whole standard

One pass from start to report, using the O2 cylinder → valve → tube → mask, as home long-term oxygen therapy for an adult.

  1. Document the device use specification and durations (Figure 2 entry point, ISO 14971:2019, 5.2). Home LTOT, adult, used for months → long-term exposure, ≥ 30 d (5.1 d) 3)).
  2. Fix the gas pathway boundary (3.11). Inner surfaces of the valve downstream of its inlet, the tube lumen, the mask interior. Exclude the O2 supply itself (Clause 1) and the mask cushion (3.11 Note 3 → ISO 10993).
  3. Identify biocompatibility hazards (4.3, MAPS DOPS). Materials of valve seat, tube polymer, mask body; additives and colourants; particle shedding; off-gassing; degradation from flexing and UV. Remember 4.3 NOTE 1: the mask may need both 18562-1 and 10993-1.
  4. Size the assessment (4.4, H·R·R). Analyse those hazards, determine the risk each poses, document both in the report, and let duration and frequency set the rigour — which produces the categorization used in step 1.
  5. Write the biological evaluation plan (4.5, MAP + Gaps). Formulation, additives, process aids for each part; what is known; and the gaps to be filled.
  6. Run the three credibility gates (Figure 2 / Clause 5):
    • PM → credible → condition, test to Part 2, report. Patient-group adjustment not applied (6.1 d), US EPA limits).
    • VOS → credible → condition, test to Part 3, report. Convert concentration to inhalation dose using the breathed volume (6.1 e)).
    • Condensate → dry cylinder O2, nothing to condense → not credible → supply rationale, document outcome (5.4 a)'s three conditions all fail).
  7. Pick the patient row (6.2 + Table 1). Adult-only IFU → 60 kg, 11,5 m³/d resting. Ambulant homecare adult → apply EXAMPLE 3 and use 19,7 m³/d. If the IFU ever claims infants, 6.2 b) forces 3,5 kg, 2,3 m³/d.
  8. Derive a limit for each VOS (Clause 7, Figure 3, long-term branch): published long-term inhalational limit → else ISO 10993-17 to get TI then TE (parenteral data free, other routes need justifying) → else 2 µg/m³.
  9. Compare dose against limit (4.5 b)). Dose below TE → risk negligible, document the decision in the report, done. Dose above → risk control (Clause 9): revise shape, materials or processes and repeat the evaluation.
  10. If no practicable fix → benefit-risk analysis (Clause 10, ISO 14971:2019, 7.4). If benefits win: disclose the residual risk in the accompanying information (10 c)) and document the analysis in the report (10 d)).
  11. Write the biological evaluation report (Clause 11) with its six elements — remembering items 1 to 4 are owed even if nothing was tested.
  12. After release: post-market surveillance continues (4.5 g), though its plan does not belong in the biological evaluation plan), and re-evaluate on any of the five triggers in 4.7 a).

RECAP

Cheat sheet and the mnemonic index

The one idea
Protect the patient from substances carried by the gas, not by contact. Pathway = interior surfaces gas is inspired over, bounded by the entry ports.
3 routes, 3 parts
PM → Part 2 · VOS → Part 3 · leachables in condensate → Part 4.
3 durations
Limited ≤ 24 h · Prolonged > 1 d and < 30 d · Long-term ≥ 30 d — all measured as total exposure period (3.39).
Limits ladder
Published limit → else ISO 10993-17 TI then TE → else default: 120 µg/d short, 2 µg/m³ long-term VOS, Table 2 TTC for condensate.
Data or no data
Data exists → TE. No data → TTC. TI is the per-kg intermediate: TE = TI × body mass.
Patient group
Lowest intended body mass (condensate) or breathing volume (VOS), Table 1. Not applied to PM — EPA limits.
Scaling rule
Derived TEs scale by body mass. Fallback values never do — not the TTC, not 120 µg/d, not 2 µg/m³.
Evaluation ≠ testing
Every device is evaluated. A rationale, five-condition equivalence, or safe-use history can replace a test (4.5 d)).
Type tests
Before release or a change only. No ongoing production testing (4.2 c)).
If over the limit
Risk control (revise and re-evaluate) → else benefit-risk (ISO 14971:2019, 7.4) → disclose residual risk in the IFU.
Prove conformity
Plan + risk management file + biological evaluation report (the 6 elements of Clause 11). Clauses 9 and 10: risk management plan and file.
Re-evaluate when
Formulation · Processing · Storage IFU · Intended use · Anything the risk process flags (4.7 a)).

SELF-TEST

Thirty-four questions with answers

Answer before expanding. Questions are grouped by area and weighted towards the details that summaries lose.

Scope, front matter and terms
Name the four things ISO 18562-1 explicitly does not cover.
Biological hazards arising from mechanical damage; biological evaluation of surfaces with direct contact with the patient or user (those go to the ISO 10993 series); contamination already present in the gas supplied from the gas sources during normal use; and abnormal use. Note that use error is covered, because it sits inside normal use.
Which is broader, intended use or normal use — and where does use error sit?
Normal use is broader. Intended use (3.15) focuses on the medical purpose; normal use (3.21) adds maintenance, service, transport, stand-by, routine inspection and adjustments. Use error occurs within normal use (3.21 Note 2 and Figure 1), which is why this document covers it while excluding abnormal use.
How many terms are defined, and why does Annex D appear to have more?
44 terms, 3.1 to 3.44. Annex D has more rows because synonyms and abbreviations get their own alphabetical entries pointing at a shared clause — regulatory authority (3.4), essential principles of safety and performance (3.7), expected service life (3.8), particulates and PM (3.22), plus SVOC, VOC, VVOC, VOS, TE, TI and TTC.
Distinguish process (3.24) from processing (3.25).
Process is generic: a set of interrelated or interacting activities using inputs to deliver an intended result — as in “risk management process”. Processing is specific: activity to prepare a new or used device for its intended use, i.e. cleaning, disinfection, sterilization; some authorities call processing between uses reprocessing. In 4.7 a) 1) and a) 2), “processing” means 3.25.
What does formulation (3.10) deliberately exclude, and which clause compensates?
It excludes processing aids, mould release agents, residual contaminants and other manufacturing aids not intended to be part of the material. 4.5 a) 1) compensates by requiring the plan to detail formulation and additives and process aids — otherwise process aids would fall through the gap.
Give the three boiling-point bands, and say what defines VOS instead.
VVOC 0–50 °C, VOC 50–250 °C, SVOC ≥ 250 °C, all at 101,3 kPa. VOS is the umbrella term and is defined differently — any organic substance whose saturation pressure at 20 °C exceeds the current analytical detection threshold — and it includes all three bands.
How is total exposure period (3.39) measured, and what does it deliberately ignore?
In days between first and last use of the device or a replacement of it. It counts elapsed calendar days whether or not the device is used every day, and regardless of the duration of exposure on each day. Under infrequent use (3.13), it is determined for a single treatment episode instead.
Clause 4
Recite the seven items of 4.1 e), and name the sub-requirement hidden in item 4.
MOACEHT: physical/chemical characteristics of candidate materials over the expected lifetime; effects of operation, handling and environment over the expected service life; aging and environment during storage over the shelf-life; effects of cleaning, disinfection and sterilisation; exposure to rated environmental conditions; any history of human exposure data; existing toxicology and biocompatibility data on materials, breakdown products and metabolites. Item 4's sub-item i) is two-sided: consider the agents' impact on degradation and methods that add potentially harmful substances to the pathway.
A product was assessed under the 2017 edition. What does 4.1 f) require now?
The series shall not be used to mandate re-testing — but conformity to the new edition shall still be shown by providing a justification for omission of further testing, and a history of safe clinical use can document why extra testing is not required. However, if any of the changes described in ISO 10993-1:2018, 4.9 occur, an evaluation of the biological risks of that change shall be performed using the current version.
When are type tests required, and when are they not?
4.2 c): only prior to releasing a product, or a change to a product, for production. There is no requirement for ongoing testing throughout the production lifetime. It is a gate at release and at change, not a batch obligation.
You test a representative sample instead of the final device. What do you owe?
Two things. 4.2 a): consideration of whether the differences could affect the test results. 4.2 b): a description of any differences and a detailed rationale for why each difference is not expected to impact biocompatibility. Some authorities evaluate these rationales (NOTE 2), and Annex A.2 adds that choosing which variant to test also requires justification.
List the eight items of 4.3 c).
MAPS DOPS: 1) material(s) of manufacture (with toxicological fitness for purpose in selection); 2) intended additives, process contaminants and residues; 3) substances or particles released in normal use; 4) degradation products during shelf-life and expected service life; 5) other components and their interactions; 6) performance and characteristics of the final device; 7) physical characteristics including size, porosity, shape; 8) effects of any processing steps before or between uses.
Why might you have to test without humidification?
Two reasons from 4.3. NOTE 2: tests are done on the worst case configuration, which can mean testing with and without heat and humidification to establish which is worse. NOTE 3: humidified air can affect the measurements of VOS and particulates, so dry testing may be needed for measurement validity, not just severity.
Can the ISO 18562 tests evaluate degradation?
No. 4.3 NOTE 4: the tests of the ISO 18562 series cannot alone evaluate degradation. Item 4 of 4.3 c) must be answered with reasoning and other evidence — ISO 10993-9 (degradation products framework) sits in the bibliography for this purpose.
What two things must 4.4 put in the biological evaluation report, and what drives the required rigour?
The results of the hazard analysis (4.4 a) 1)) and the results of the risk determination for each hazard (4.4 b) 1)). Rigour is principally determined by the duration and frequency of exposure and the hazards identified; supporting information must consider material physical/chemical characteristics, the device's electromechanical nature, and the frequency, duration and conditions of exposure. This enables the categorization of uses, which feeds test selection.
A hazard is identified but the dose is below the tolerable exposure. What now?
4.5 b): the risk can be shown to be negligible, so no further work on that hazard is required — but b) 1) the decision shall be documented in the biological evaluation report. Negligible does not mean silent.
State the five conditions of the equivalence EXAMPLE in 4.5 d).
Same intended use; demonstrable similarity in a specified function and physical form; identical formulation; contains no additional chemicals; uses the same manufacturing processes — such that it is equivalent to a device already evaluated as biocompatible. Then it might not require additional testing. And per 4.5 d) 2) i), appropriate evidence can also include a history of safe clinical use or testing / toxicological risk assessment conducted before this document was published.
Does 4.5 e) require chemical characterization for every gas pathway?
No — it is scoped to gas pathways that can contact liquids. For those, identification of material chemical constituents and consideration of chemical characterization shall be undertaken, and only if substances are found lacking sufficient toxicological data should biological testing be considered. NOTE 2 adds that local effects (cytotoxicity, irritation, sensitization) may still need biological testing and are evaluated as part of ISO 18562-4, while systemic effects can often be handled by chemical characterization and risk assessment.
Is post-market surveillance required, and does its plan go in the biological evaluation plan?
4.5 g): test results cannot guarantee freedom from hazards, so production release shall be followed by post-market surveillance. But post-market surveillance plans are not required in the biological evaluation plan. Required activity, excluded document.
Name the four hazards in 4.5 NOTE 4 with their trigger conditions.
OCLL: ozone — for pathways in contact with active electromechanical or electrostatic parts in normal condition; CO, NOx and CO2 — where inorganic gases are added, generated or concentrated; leachables — for pathways in contact with anaesthetic agents where the gas can be inspired in normal condition; leachables — for pathways in contact with substances intended to be delivered via the respiratory tract, e.g. inhalational drugs. The series does not currently address them, but some authorities having jurisdiction require the manufacturer to evaluate them.
Give the three test-duration rules of 4.6 e) 3).
i) clinical use ≤ 24 h → test duration at least as long as the clinical duration of use. ii) device could be used multiple times in 24 h (with or without processing) → use the worst-case cumulative use time as the test duration. iii) clinical use > 24 h → test at least 24 h and adequate to represent the release of substances over the expected duration of use.
What is the documentation standard for test data in 4.6?
4.6 f) 2): test data complete to the extent that an independent analysis could be made shall be documented in the biological evaluation report. The completeness bar — independent re-analysis — is the requirement, not merely “include the results”.
List the five re-evaluation triggers of 4.7 a).
FPSIA: 1) any change in the formulation of materials used in manufacture, processing or primary packaging; 2) change in processing methods, including sterilization; 3) any change in the IFU concerning storage (e.g. shelf-life or transport); 4) any change in the intended use; 5) any other changes identified by the risk management process.
One component of a complex device changes. What structure does 4.7 b) impose?
One mandatory item plus a choice of four. Mandatory: b) 1) information, e.g. a rationale, supporting that the biocompatibility of the other components is not affected. Then b) 2) one of: i) evidence the new materials show no increased biocompatibility concerns versus the old; ii) test and evaluate the new components per this document; iii) comparison to identical components previously tested and used in a comparable application; iv) re-test and re-evaluate the complete product. And per 4.7 c), multiple changes are assessed separately and in combination.
Clauses 5 to 11 and the annexes
A single-use breathing tube is replaced daily for 40 days on one patient. Which category?
Long-term exposure (≥ 30 d). Per 5.1 c), where components are replaced every few days, the multiple sequential exposures to new replacements shall be considered for the total exposure period — that is the standard's own EXAMPLE 1. Replacing the consumable resets the component, not the patient's exposure period.
Which route gets no patient-group adjustment, and where do its limits come from?
Particulates (ISO 18562-2). Per 6.1 d), adjustments for patient groups are not applied, because allowable limits are taken from US EPA 40 CFR Part 50 — an ambient air quality standard rather than a per-patient toxicological calculation.
Derive the 19,7 m³/d figure in 6.2 EXAMPLE 3.
Homecare ventilator, ventilator-dependent adult, up to 2 h/day exercise. Exercise: 2 h × 4,6 m³/h = 9,2 m³. Rest of the day: 11,5 × 22/24 = 10,5 m³. Total 19,7 m³, up from 11,5. The resting volume is pro-rated over the remaining 22 hours — not simply added to the exercise volume.
No toxicological data for a VOS. What limit applies, and does body mass matter?
Depends on duration. Limited or prolonged (< 30 d): 120 µg/d, from ICH M7:2023-09, Table 2. Long-term (≥ 30 d): 2 µg/m³, from ISO 16000-6:2021. Neither shall be scaled according to body mass — and note the unit difference: the first is a dose, the second a concentration. Inhalation is regarded as systemic exposure.
Adult TE for a condensate leachable is 100 µg/d. What is the infant TE, and why?
5,8 µg/d. Per 8.2 a), non-adult TEs are adjusted for lower body mass; the standard's own example gives the infant factor as 3,5 kg ÷ 60 kg = 0,058. So 100 × 0,058 = 5,8 µg/d. This scaling applies to a derived TE — it must not be applied to a Table 2 TTC or to the Clause 7 defaults.
A device is used for more than 30 days but only ever on premature neonates or infants. What relief does 8.2 b) give?
For TE calculations a premature neonate or infant only remains in that category for 6 months, so such a device may be evaluated on a 1-year total exposure period instead of the lifetime exposure period. In Table 2 terms that moves you from the > 10 years / 1,5 µg/d row to the ≤ 1 year / 20 µg/d row. It applies only if the device is intended exclusively for those patients.
A dose exceeds its limit and no practicable alternative exists. Walk the rest of the path.
Clause 9 a): review materials and manufacture. Risk control practicable → revise the design and repeat the biocompatibility evaluation. Not practicable → Clause 10: benefit-risk analysis per ISO 14971:2019, 7.4. If benefits outweigh risks, the device conforms (9 a) 3)) — but the manufacturer shall disclose the residual risk in the accompanying information (10 c)) and document the analysis in the biological evaluation report (10 d)).
Which of Clause 11's six report elements survive when no testing was performed?
Items 1 to 4 are unconditional: device description; the biological evaluation plan; description and rationale for test articles plus any differences from the final device; and the results of the risk assessment (see 4.4). Item 5 is conditional on testing having been performed, and item 6 on a benefit-risk analysis having been performed. A device that needed no testing still owes a full report.
Where do 120 µg/d and 2 µg/m³ come from, and what is the boundary on their use?
Per Annex A.2: 120 µg/d is the lower of two candidates — the 5th percentile of noncancer TIs from inhalation NOAEL/LOAEL values for ≤ 30 d (135 µg/d) and the acceptable intake of an individual mutagenic impurity in a pharmaceutical for ≤ 30 d (120 µg/d, ICH M7:2023-09) — chosen to be protective for both cancer and noncancer effects. 2 µg/m³ is the ISO 16000 non-targeted reporting limit for indoor air, considered safe for permanent exposure but too restrictive for limited and prolonged durations. Boundary: these limits apply only to substances added by the medical device to gases supplied to patients, and are not relevant to any other type of patient exposure.
When is “use the smallest patient” not conservative?
Per Annex A.2 on 6.1: when the flow rate through the device does not correspond directly with the patient's breathed volume — either the patient breathes some gas independently of the device, or therapy needs more flow than reaches the lungs. If the ratio of flow rate to breathed volume differs significantly between patient groups, it can be necessary to calculate the inhalation dose for all applicable patient groups. The shortcut in 6.2 b) assumes a comparable ratio.
